\begin{abstract}
\noindent Mattingly et al.\ (2025) show in a 19-country experiment that short videos from China's state broadcaster CGTN substantially increase preference for China's political and economic models over those of the United States. I reproduce all 24 of their primary estimates at the published precision and then exploit a layer of randomization the paper does not analyze: which videos each respondent saw within a treatment arm. Individual messages differ a lot: among respondents who saw a single CGTN video, the most and least persuasive differ by \val{range.pm.1} points on China's political model and nearly one point on its economic model (six-point scales). In two independent designs, the economic videos increase preference for China's economic model relative to \vid{china\_pol1}. The corresponding differences on the political outcome are statistically inconclusive. The within-arm assignment also departed from the published design: every respondent in the China arm saw the same political video, the least persuasive of the four CGTN videos in the Competition arm.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Can an authoritarian state persuade foreign publics that its model of governance is superior? Mattingly, Incerti, Ju, Moreshead, Tanaka, and Yamagishi (2025) answer this question with a large preregistered experiment. In June 2022, they showed \val{n.randomized} respondents in 19 countries short videos produced by China's state broadcaster CGTN, by the US State Department, by both, or neither, and then asked whether they preferred the Chinese or the American political and economic model. The effects are large. Two CGTN videos raise the share of respondents preferring China's political model from \val{share.pm.control.0} to \val{share.pm.china.0} percent. US videos move respondents toward the American model, but by less. And when respondents see one video from each country, opinion still shifts toward China.

Each treatment in the experiment is a \emph{bundle} of two videos, and the paper estimates the effect of each bundle as a whole. In their conclusion, the authors identify ``unbundling'' the treatments, and theorizing about which specific messages are most persuasive, as a priority for future research. This paper takes a first step in that direction using the original data.

The question connects the study to two literatures. Research on authoritarian propaganda asks when and how state media persuade (e.g., Carter and Carter 2023), and Mattingly et al.\ extend that question to foreign audiences. Research on experimental design and persuasion warns that effects estimated with a few fixed stimuli may not generalize to the wider population of messages a source produces, because stimuli differ in ways the researcher did not intend (Wells and Windschitl 1999; Clifford, Leeper, and Rainey 2023). Persuasive effects tend to be similar across types of people (Coppock 2023) but can vary considerably across messages, which is why testing many messages pays off for campaigns (Hewitt et al.\ 2024). In pairwise judgments of argument strength, arguments of the same rhetorical type vary widely in persuasiveness across issues, while average differences between types are modest (Blumenau and Lauderdale 2024). If the same is true of state media, the effect of ``CGTN'' is better understood as a distribution over messages than as a single number.

The paper makes two contributions. The first is a replication. I re-implement the authors' primary analysis in Python, starting from their analysis dataset, and reproduce all 24 coefficients and standard errors in their primary results table exactly at the reported precision. Along the way I document how the analysis sample is constructed and several details that matter when moving the analysis from R to Python.

The second contribution is an extension. The experiment randomized not only which arm a respondent entered but also which videos they saw within it. I show that this within-arm assignment has a specific structure. In the Competition arm, one Chinese and one US video were drawn independently from each country's four videos, forming a four-by-four factorial design in which each respondent saw a single CGTN video. In the China and US arms, every respondent saw the same political video, and only its companion was randomized. The authors report that the videos within each arm were drawn at random, and the observed assignment frequencies and balance tests are consistent with this (Section~\ref{sec:assignment}). Under that assignment, comparisons across videos within an arm identify causal effects just as the paper's comparisons across arms do.

Individual CGTN messages differ in persuasiveness by more than the Competition arm's overall effect, and the differences do not line up with economic versus political content. Economic videos hold up well against the one political video they can be compared with in both designs. And the China arm always included the CGTN video that did worst in the Competition arm, so the paper's headline estimate may understate what other combinations of CGTN content could do; testing that would take a new experiment, since videos may complement or substitute for one another. These comparisons rest on few videos and smaller subsamples, so I report minimum detectable effects and multiple-comparison-adjusted $p$-values throughout.

\section{The Original Study}

\paragraph{Argument.} Mattingly et al.\ (2025) argue that audiences evaluate political systems not only on democratic values but also on government performance: economic growth, stability, competent leadership, and responsiveness. Chinese state media emphasize exactly these dimensions. A second part of the argument concerns prior beliefs. Foreign audiences typically know much more about the United States than about China, so their beliefs about China are weaker and more easily moved by new information.

\paragraph{Design.} The experiment was fielded on Lucid between June 10 and 19, 2022, in 19 English-, Spanish-, and Arabic-speaking countries across Africa, Asia, Europe, North America, Oceania, Latin America, and the Middle East. Respondents had to pass two pre-treatment attention checks. They were then randomly assigned, within country, with equal probability to one of four arms:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
  \item \textbf{Control}: two nature videos;
  \item \textbf{China}: two of four CGTN videos;
  \item \textbf{USA}: two of four US State Department videos;
  \item \textbf{Competition}: one CGTN video and one US video.
\end{itemize}
Each country's video pool contained two videos promoting its political model and two promoting its economic model. In the single-country arms, every respondent was to see at least one political video (Mattingly et al.\ 2025, footnote 9). The authors selected these videos from large corpora of CGTN and State Department content which they characterize with topic models, and use text-based distance measures to show that the selected videos are representative of what each broadcaster produces. Section~\ref{sec:videos} describes the eight videos.

\paragraph{Outcomes and estimation.} The primary outcomes are two six-point scales asking whether respondents prefer the US or the Chinese political model and economic model (1 = strongly prefer the United States, 6 = strongly prefer China); a third asks which country respondents would like to be the world leader. The authors estimate effects by OLS with HC2 robust standard errors, both without covariates and with country fixed effects plus gender, age, education, national pride, and left--right ideology. Missing covariate values are imputed by predictive mean matching.

\paragraph{Findings.} All three treatments have large and precisely estimated effects (Table~\ref{tab:replication}). The China treatment raises preference for China's political model by \val{adj.china.pm} points and for its economic model by \val{adj.china.em} points. The US treatment lowers them by \val{adj.usa.pm.neg} and \val{adj.usa.em.neg} points. When the two compete, the net effect still favors China (\val{adj.comp.pm} and \val{adj.comp.em} points). Mechanism analyses suggest that CGTN videos work mainly by improving perceptions of China's performance rather than of its democratic qualities.

\section{Replication}

\subsection{Data and Sample}

The replication package includes the raw Qualtrics exports, the authors' cleaning script, and the cleaned analysis dataset. I start from the cleaned dataset rather than the raw exports for one reason. The authors impute missing covariates with the R package \texttt{mice}, and the imputed values depend on R's random number generator. They cannot be reproduced exactly in Python, so re-running the imputation would produce small differences in the covariate-adjusted estimates that reflect software rather than substance. The cleaned file reproduces the paper's arm sizes (1,558, 1,622, 1,565, and 1,531) and outcome sample sizes, which the code checks automatically.

The cleaned file contains \val{n.total} respondents, of whom \val{n.randomized} were randomized. Table~\ref{tab:flow} shows where the others left the survey. The categories are mutually exclusive and follow the order of the questionnaire. More than two in five respondents who completed the attention checks failed them, so the analysis sample is selected toward attentive respondents. This selection happens before randomization, so it does not threaten the comparison between arms, but it does define the population to which the estimates apply.

\subsection{Specification}

I estimate, separately for each outcome $Y_i$,
\begin{equation}
Y_i = \alpha + \beta_1\,\text{China}_i + \beta_2\,\text{USA}_i + \beta_3\,\text{Competition}_i + \mathbf{X}_i'\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \varepsilon_i,
\label{eq:main}
\end{equation}
where Control is the omitted category and $\mathbf{X}_i$ contains country fixed effects and the five covariates entered linearly, exactly as in the authors' code. The unadjusted model omits $\mathbf{X}_i$. Standard errors are HC2, the default of the \texttt{estimatr} package the authors use. With only treatment indicators on the right-hand side, HC2 coincides with the conservative Neyman variance estimator for a difference in means (Samii and Aronow 2012).

Some details of the port are easy to get wrong. The Python reader for R data files does not keep the order of R factor levels: it sorts the treatment arms alphabetically, which would silently make China the reference category instead of Control, so I restore the original order explicitly. The country identifier has to stay a string. In both R and Python, the reference country for string identifiers is the first in lexical order (code ``10'', Kenya), which fixes the constant in the adjusted model. And the standard error type has to be set explicitly, because Python's default is not HC2.

Every coefficient, standard error, and sample size is identical to the published Table A3 when rounded to two decimals, as are the eight estimates for the world-leader outcome that the table omits; an automated cell-by-cell comparison of all 24 is included in the code repository. \section{Extension: Unbundling the Treatments}

\subsection{Motivation}

The replication confirms that a bundle of two CGTN videos is highly persuasive. It does not tell us which content does the work. The published paper and its appendix report effects only by arm.\footnote{The authors' replication code (\texttt{7.\ mechanism\_analysis.R}) contains an unreported regression of the outcomes on the type of each respondent's first-listed video. Appendix B reproduces it, shows that it pools Competition-arm respondents with the USA arm, and re-estimates it with the arms kept separate.} If the effect operates through performance information, as the authors argue, then videos about economic achievement might be especially effective, perhaps even for views about the political model. Alternatively, persuasion might depend on idiosyncratic features of particular videos, in which case the effect of ``Chinese state media'' would depend heavily on which messages audiences happen to see.

\subsection{The Videos}
\label{sec:videos}

Table~\ref{tab:videocontent} summarizes the eight videos. All four CGTN videos argue from performance: merit-based selection of leaders, leadership turnover, growth, and poverty reduction. Three of them contrast China with the United States or the West, and two name the Communist Party explicitly. The State Department videos vary more in form, from narration to testimonials, and only one refers, in general terms, to authoritarian governments. With four videos per country, no single feature can be credibly linked to persuasiveness, and the table does not reveal one that cleanly sorts the videos by their effects. It does suggest candidate features for a designed experiment. Explicit references to the Communist Party, for example, appear in the weakest CGTN video and in the weaker of the two economic videos, under the mapping described in the table note.

\subsection{Within-Arm Randomization}
\label{sec:assignment}

The data record the identifiers of the two videos each respondent saw. Cross-tabulating them reveals the structure of the within-arm assignment. Which combinations of videos occurred, and how often, is observed directly. How the combinations were generated is not recorded, because the replication package contains no survey flow: I infer the assignment rule from the cross-tabulations and rely on the authors' statement that videos were drawn at random (Mattingly et al.\ 2025, footnote 9). Balance tests can show only that the data are consistent with random assignment, not that it occurred.

\paragraph{China and USA arms.} Table~\ref{tab:assignment} shows that the first video (\vid{video1}) was drawn with roughly equal probability from all four of the country's videos. The second (\vid{video2}) was not drawn at random: it is always \vid{pol2}, unless \vid{pol2} was the first draw, in which case it is \vid{pol1}. This rule satisfies the paper's requirement that every respondent see at least one political video, but not its description of the second video as randomly drawn from either the political or the economic pool (Mattingly et al.\ 2025, footnote 9): it fixes \emph{which} political video. As a result, every respondent in the China arm saw \vid{china\_pol2}, and every respondent in the USA arm saw \vid{us\_pol2}. The randomized \emph{companion} of \vid{pol2} is \vid{pol1} for about half of respondents and one of the two economic videos for about a quarter each. Comparing respondents whose companion was economic with those whose companion was \vid{pol1} therefore estimates the causal effect of replacing \vid{pol1} with an economic video, holding \vid{pol2} fixed. The cost is that the separate effect of \vid{pol2} cannot be identified in these arms.

\paragraph{Competition arm.} Here one US video and one Chinese video were each drawn from all four of the country's videos, independently of each other. All sixteen combinations occur, each with \val{indep.min} to \val{indep.max} respondents. A chi-squared test does not reject independence of the two assignments ($\chi^2 = \val{indep.chi2}$, $df = \val{indep.df}$, $p = \val{indep.p}$). This four-by-four factorial identifies the effect of each Chinese video relative to the others while the US video is balanced by randomization, and vice versa. It is also the only place in the experiment where respondents saw a single CGTN video, so it provides the cleanest comparison of individual messages.

\paragraph{Balance and missing outcomes.} The within-arm draws are consistent with random assignment (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:balance}). I divide respondents by video in four ways: by the first video in the China and USA arms, and by the Chinese and the US video in the Competition arm. Across these groupings, tests of equal covariate means give $p$-values between \val{bal.means.pmin} and \val{bal.means.pmax}, with one of 20 below 0.10; tests that the video is independent of country give $p$-values of \val{bal.country.pmin} or higher; and a multinomial logit of the video on all covariates and country dummies gives omnibus $p$-values between \val{bal.omnibus.pmin} and \val{bal.omnibus.pmax}. Outcomes are missing for \val{missing.min} to \val{missing.max} percent of respondents in each video group, and missingness does not differ detectably across videos ($p$-values between \val{missing.pmin} and \val{missing.pmax}), so differential attrition is unlikely to drive the comparisons below.

\paragraph{Display order.} Mattingly et al.\ report that the order of the two countries' videos in the Competition arm was randomized. The codebook labels \vid{video1} and \vid{video2} as the ``first'' and ``second'' video watched, which would imply that the US video always played first in that arm. The public files contain neither a display-order field nor the survey flow. I therefore use the fields to identify which videos were shown and interpret the Competition-arm contrasts as averages over the reported randomization of order.

\subsection{Estimation}

For the Competition arm, I regress each outcome on indicators for each video,
\begin{equation}
Y_i = \alpha + \sum_{v} \theta_v\,\text{ChinaVideo}_{vi} + \sum_{u} \phi_u\,\text{USVideo}_{ui} + \mathbf{X}_i'\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \varepsilon_i,
\label{eq:videos}
\end{equation}
where $v \in \{\vid{econ1}, \vid{econ2}, \vid{pol1}\}$ indexes the Chinese videos and $u$ the US videos, each relative to its country's \vid{pol2}. I use \vid{pol2} as the reference because it is the video held fixed in the single-country arms. A Wald test that the three $\theta_v$ are jointly zero tests whether the four Chinese videos are equally persuasive.

For the China and USA arms, I estimate within each arm
\begin{equation}
Y_i = \alpha + \delta\,\text{EconCompanion}_i + \mathbf{X}_i'\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \varepsilon_i,
\label{eq:companion}
\end{equation}
where $\delta$ is the effect of replacing \vid{pol1} with an economic video as the companion of \vid{pol2}. The Competition-arm analogue is $\tfrac{1}{2}(\theta_{\text{econ1}} + \theta_{\text{econ2}}) - \theta_{\text{pol1}}$, the difference between the average economic video and \vid{pol1}. Both contrasts compare the same content, in independent samples and under different designs. The reference is \vid{pol1} because of the design, not because of its results: in the single-country arms \vid{pol2} is always shown, so \vid{pol1} is the only political video whose assignment is randomized there, and the Competition-arm contrast uses the same reference so that the two designs can be compared. That \vid{pol1} turns out to be the stronger of the two Chinese political videos (Section~\ref{sec:messages}) makes this a demanding comparison for the economic videos.

All models use the same covariates and HC2 standard errors as the replication. Within each family of tests I report $p$-values adjusted by the Holm (1979) and Benjamini--Hochberg (1995) procedures, which the authors also use. I report the minimum detectable effect (MDE) at 80 percent power and a two-sided 5 percent test, $\text{MDE} = (z_{0.975} + z_{0.80}) \times \text{SE} \approx 2.8 \times \text{SE}$. None of the extension analyses was preregistered. To limit the scope for selective reporting, I report the effect of every video (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:videos}) and the comparison that pools each country's two political videos (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:types}) alongside the contrasts discussed in the text.

\subsection{Results: Individual Messages Differ}
\label{sec:messages}

Figure~\ref{fig:videos} and Appendix Table~\ref{tab:videos} report the effect of each video in the Competition arm. Relative to \vid{china\_pol2}, each of the other three CGTN videos raises preference for China's political model by between \val{china.pm.min} and \val{china.pm.max} points, and for its economic model by between \val{china.em.min} and \val{china.em.max} points. The four CGTN videos are clearly not equally persuasive: the joint test rejects equality for both the political model ($\chi^2_3 = \val{joint.china.pm.chi2}$) and the economic model ($\chi^2_3 = \val{joint.china.em.chi2}$; both $p < 0.001$). The ranges, \val{range.pm} and \val{range.em} points, are larger than the Competition arm's overall effects on the two outcomes (\val{adj.comp.pm} and \val{adj.comp.em}); on the economic model, the range is as large as the China arm's effect (\val{adj.china.em}). Because a range takes the extremes of four noisy estimates, it overstates the true spread on average; a parametric bootstrap puts this bias at about 0.01 points or less, with basic 95 percent intervals of \val{rangeci.pm.low} to \val{rangeci.pm.high} and \val{rangeci.em.low} to \val{rangeci.em.high}.

The differences do not reduce to content type. The weakest CGTN video is political (\vid{china\_pol2}), but the other political video, \vid{china\_pol1} (\val{v.pm.china_pol1}), is about as persuasive about the political model as the economic video \vid{china\_econ2} (\val{v.pm.china_econ2}). A comparison of economic with political CGTN videos that pools the two political videos (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:types}) therefore mixes a content difference with the weakness of one particular video.

The US videos look different. On the political model, the four State Department videos are statistically indistinguishable (joint $p = \val{joint.us.pm.p}$; range \val{range.us.pm} points). On the economic model, they differ ($p = \val{joint.us.em.p}$; Holm-adjusted $p = \val{joint.us.em.pholm}$), with both economic videos pulling respondents further toward the US model than \vid{us\_pol2}. A significant joint test for one set of videos and a weaker one for the other is not by itself evidence that the two sets differ, so I test the difference directly. The range of the four CGTN videos' effects exceeds that of the four State Department videos by \val{disp.pm} points on the political model (basic 95 percent bootstrap interval \val{disp.pm.low} to \val{disp.pm.high}) and by \val{disp.em} points on the economic model (\val{disp.em.low} to \val{disp.em.high}), and a comparison of standard deviations gives the same result. Variation across messages is thus larger for CGTN content.

\subsection{Results: Economic Videos versus \texorpdfstring{\vid{pol1}}{pol1}}
\label{sec:content}

Table~\ref{tab:content} and Figure~\ref{fig:content} compare the economic videos with \vid{pol1} in both designs. For CGTN, the two designs agree closely. On preference for China's economic model, an economic video outperforms \vid{china\_pol1} by \val{ct.china.em} points in the China arm (SE \val{ct.china.em.se}) and by \val{ct.comp.em} points in the Competition arm (SE \val{ct.comp.em.se}); both survive the Holm correction across the eight tests in the table. On preference for China's political model, the differences are \val{ct.china.pm} and \val{ct.comp.pm} points, with confidence intervals of roughly $\pm \val{ct.china.pm.half}$ and $\pm \val{ct.comp.pm.half}$. The pattern for CGTN is asymmetric. If each video persuaded mainly within its own domain, \vid{china\_pol1} should outperform the economic videos on the political outcome. It does not detectably do so, while the economic videos clearly outperform it on the economic outcome. This is consistent with the authors' performance-legitimacy argument, in which evidence of economic success also makes the political system that produced it look more attractive. Both designs, however, identify relative effects. The China-arm contrast holds \vid{china\_pol2} fixed, and the Competition-arm contrast holds a US video fixed, so neither shows how much an economic video moves views of China's political model compared with seeing no CGTN video at all.\footnote{A bound is available under a weak assumption. If \vid{china\_pol2}, a pro-China video, does not reduce preference for China's political model, then each economic video's effect relative to watching the US video alone is at least its effect relative to \vid{china\_pol2}: \val{v.pm.china_econ2} and \val{v.pm.china_econ1} points (Table~\ref{tab:videos}). The point estimates on the economic outcome diverge somewhat (\val{ct.state.usa.em} in the USA arm and \val{ct.state.comp.em} in the Competition arm). Given standard errors of \val{ct.state.se.min} to \val{ct.state.se.max}, the gap may be noise, or US economic messages may matter more when they compete directly with Chinese ones.

The position checks give no sign that video position drives the single-country estimates. If, as the codebook states, \vid{video1} is the first video shown, economic companions always played before \vid{pol2}, while \vid{pol1} did so only half the time. Among China-arm respondents for whom \vid{china\_pol2} was the second video, the economic-versus-\vid{pol1} contrast is \val{pos.econ.pm} (SE \val{pos.econ.pm.se}) on the political model and \val{pos.econ.em} (SE \val{pos.econ.em.se}) on the economic model, close to the full-sample estimates. Among respondents who saw two political videos, the order of \vid{pol1} and \vid{pol2} makes no detectable difference (\val{pos.order.pm}, SE \val{pos.order.pm.se}, and \val{pos.order.em}, SE \val{pos.order.em.se}), although with these standard errors an order effect of about \val{pos.order.mde} points could go undetected. The USA arm shows the same pattern (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:position}).

\subsection{Exploratory Results: What Do the Videos Change?}

The authors measure eight perceptions of China after treatment, on seven-point agreement scales, and use them to show that CGTN videos work mainly through perceived performance. I apply the same contrasts to these perceptions. Because the perceptions are themselves outcomes of treatment rather than measured mediators, these results describe what each kind of video changes, not a causal pathway, and I treat them as exploratory.

Figure~\ref{fig:mechanisms} shows the economic-versus-\vid{pol1} contrast for each perception. Two contrasts hold up in both designs. Economic videos do not raise belief that China's government delivers economic growth any more than \vid{china\_pol1} does: the differences are \val{mech.growth.china} (SE \val{mech.growth.china.se}) and \val{mech.growth.comp} (SE \val{mech.growth.comp.se}). This belief is already the most widely held of the eight in the control group (mean \val{base.growth.mean}; \val{base.growth.agree} percent agree or strongly agree). A ceiling is not the whole explanation, however: beliefs that the United States delivers growth are about as high at baseline (mean \val{us.growth.mean}), yet in the USA arm an economic companion raises them by \val{us.growth} points (SE \val{us.growth.se}; $p = \val{us.growth.p}$, or \val{us.growth.pholm} after Holm correction across the eight items). In the other direction, \vid{china\_pol1} raises belief that China's political system selects competent leaders much more than the economic videos do, by \val{mech.competent.china} (SE \val{mech.competent.china.se}) and \val{mech.competent.comp} (SE \val{mech.competent.comp.se}) points; both differences survive Holm correction across the 16 tests. None of the other contrasts does.

The perceptions therefore do not track the outcome differences in a simple way. The political video that most improves perceived leadership competence has no statistically detectable advantage over the economic videos on the political outcome. The economic videos' advantage on the economic outcome is not accompanied by larger changes in beliefs about growth. There are a few possible explanations. Economic videos may prime China's economic record, which respondents already rate highly, when they evaluate its economic model (Iyengar and Kinder 1987). They may instead teach respondents about aspects of China's economy that the survey does not measure, which would be learning rather than priming (Lenz 2009). And beliefs about growth respond to political as well as economic videos: relative to \vid{china\_pol2}, all three other CGTN videos raise them, by \val{growth.videos.min} to \val{growth.videos.max} points.

Finally, the perceptions show that the weakness of \vid{china\_pol2} extends beyond the two outcomes. Because the eight items are correlated (an average correlation of \val{index.corr} in the control group) and every comparison shares \vid{china\_pol2} as the reference, item-by-item results are not independent evidence, so I summarize them with a standardized index (Kling, Liebman, and Katz 2007; Anderson 2008). On this index, the other three CGTN videos score \val{index.avg} standard deviations higher than \vid{china\_pol2} on average (SE \val{index.avg.se}; \val{index.avg.inverse} with inverse-covariance weights), and each is individually higher at $p < 0.01$ (largest $p = \val{index.largest.p}$; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:mechvideos}). Dropping each of the eight items in turn leaves this gap between \val{loo.min} and \val{loo.max} standard deviations (all $p < 0.001$), and each video stays higher than \vid{china\_pol2} at $p < 0.05$. So the video's weakness is general, not confined to one dimension. What makes it weak is another matter, and these data cannot say: its framing or production, its credibility, how easy it is to follow, or specific content such as its explicit references to the Communist Party. Under the mapping inferred in Table~\ref{tab:videocontent}, \vid{china\_pol1} covers much the same broad subject, merit-based selection of leaders, and is far more persuasive, which suggests that the subject alone does not account for the difference.

\section{Discussion}

The original study shows that Chinese state media can shift global opinion. The extension shows that how far it shifts depends on \emph{what} it says.

The variation across messages is the main result. The four CGTN videos differ by more than the Competition arm's overall effect, and the differences do not follow broad content categories, which fits evidence that an argument's rhetorical type is a weak guide to how persuasive it is (Blumenau and Lauderdale 2024). CGTN videos also vary more than State Department videos, by a margin a direct test distinguishes from zero. An estimate from a handful of fixed stimuli is an estimate for those stimuli; claims about ``CGTN content'' need a larger sample of it (Wells and Windschitl 1999; Clifford, Leeper, and Rainey 2023).

The authors' performance-legitimacy account offers one interpretation of the content comparison. Information about economic performance could shape how viewers judge the political system behind it. The estimates compare specific videos in the presence of a second video. The perception results leave open whether viewers drew on existing beliefs about the economy or learned something the survey did not measure.

The design as implemented also matters for reading the headline estimate. Every respondent in the China arm saw \vid{china\_pol2}, because of how the at-least-one-political-video rule was implemented, not because the published design called for it. The China-arm effect is a valid estimate for the bundles actually shown. Since \vid{china\_pol2} was the weakest CGTN video in the Competition arm, other combinations might have done better. That is a hypothesis, not a result: the video was weakest next to a US video and need not be weakest next to another CGTN video, and no China-arm respondent saw a pair without it.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The extension relies on four videos per country, which differ in style, speakers, and subject as well as content type (Table~\ref{tab:videocontent}). Within-arm subsamples are a quarter to a half the size of the arms, so MDEs of \val{mde.min} to \val{mde.max} points mean that modest differences may go undetected. The perception measures are post-treatment outcomes and cover only a few beliefs about China's economy, so they cannot distinguish priming from learning. The assignment rule and the mapping from transcripts to video identifiers are inferred from the public files. Competition-arm display order is unavailable.

\paragraph{Future directions.} The obvious next step is to study message features directly. One could sample a larger number of CGTN videos, code them for performance claims, explicit references to the Communist Party, criticism of the United States, use of statistics, and production quality, and randomize those features in a new experiment. Randomizing pairs of CGTN videos would also show whether messages complement or substitute for one another, and thus whether the paper's headline estimate is conservative. Measuring both beliefs and the weight respondents place on each dimension would distinguish learning from priming. Recovering the display order from the original survey platform would also make it possible to test whether the first or the last message has the advantage when Chinese and US narratives compete.

\subsection*{B. The Replication Code's Video-Type Analysis}

The authors' replication code (\texttt{7.\ mechanism\_analysis.R}, section ``Estimate effects by treatment video'') regresses each outcome on the type of the first-listed video (\vid{video1\_clean}: China economic, China political, US economic, or US political, with Control as the reference) and tests the difference between Chinese economic and political videos. Neither the published article nor its appendix reports these estimates.

Two features of this specification matter. In the China arm, the first-listed video is the randomized draw and the second is almost always \vid{china\_pol2} (Table~\ref{tab:assignment}). ``China economic'' therefore means an economic video plus \vid{china\_pol2}, and ``China political'' means \vid{china\_pol1} plus \vid{china\_pol2}; the contrast between them is the same quantity as $\delta$ in Equation~\ref{eq:companion}. In the Competition arm the first-listed video is always the US video, so Competition respondents, who also saw a CGTN video, are pooled into the US categories with the USA arm.

Table~\ref{tab:authors} reproduces the specification and re-estimates it with Competition respondents in separate categories. The Chinese estimates are unchanged, and the pooled contrast between Chinese economic and political videos (\val{au.chinadiff.em} on the economic model) is close to the China-arm estimate in Table~\ref{tab:content} (\val{ct.china.em.3}); the two differ slightly because the pooled specification estimates the covariate coefficients and country effects on all arms rather than on the China arm alone. The US estimates, however, are strongly attenuated by the pooling: relative to Control, US videos appear to have almost no effect on preference for the political model (\val{au.pooled.usecon} and \val{au.pooled.uspol}), whereas the USA arm alone shows effects of \val{au.byarm.usecon} and \val{au.byarm.uspol}. The contrast between US economic and political videos is not significant in either specification, so the pooling distorts the levels but not the comparison of content types.
